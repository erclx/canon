# Explanation reference

Read this before choosing what a lesson covers and again while writing it. `pedagogy.md` picks what to teach next and `lesson-craft.md` builds the quiz, while this file decides what goes into the lesson and how it is explained.

Each section names the research behind it or says it is house judgment. `adopted.md` beside this file names every source, and the passages relied on sit in the skill's ledger.

## Start from the goal

A lesson planned from its content covers whatever the subject's order reaches next. Planned from its goal, it covers what the learner needs and stops.

- State what the learner will be able to do once the lesson lands, before choosing any content. A goal written after the content only describes the content.
- Write the check that would show the goal was met, being the quiz and the teach-back, before writing the explanation they test
- Cut any content the check does not need, however true or interesting it is

Wiggins and McTighe 2012 back planning from the desired result, then the evidence, then the learning plan. Mayer 2023's coherence principle backs the cut, since extraneous material costs the attention the essential material needed.

## Pair figures with words

A key process carried in prose alone makes the learner build the picture, and a figure with no words leaves them guessing what it shows.

- Carry each key process or concept in a figure paired with words, rather than in prose alone
- Put the labels on the figure's own elements, never in a legend or the paragraph beside it
- Place the caption directly under the figure it explains

The IES practice guide's Recommendation 3, rated moderate, backs pairing graphics with words and placing the words near the elements they describe. Mayer 2023's spatial contiguity principle backs the same placement.

## Signal the structure

A learner who can see how the material is organized spends no effort working it out.

- Give each idea its own heading, so the lesson's organization reads before its content does
- Set the one hard part apart from the rest, so a returning reader finds it without rereading the lesson

Mayer 2023's signaling principle backs both. Drawing the hard part as one callout is a presentation call, in `presentation.md`.

## Refute the misconception

A correct explanation read beside an unspoken wrong idea leaves the wrong idea standing. Naming it first gives the correction something to replace.

- State the misconception before the correct idea, in the words a learner holding it would use
- Say why the misconception fails, then what replaces it, rather than stating the correct idea alone
- Take the misconception from the learning record when one exists, since a wrong answer the learner gave beats one the session imagines

Schroeder, Kucera and Adesope 2022 back refutation text across 44 comparisons, with a moderate effect. Taking the misconception from the record is house judgment.

## Names first, then segments

A term met for the first time mid-explanation splits the learner's attention between the word and the idea it names.

- Name and define the key terms before the explanation that uses them. The line stating what the lesson assumes begins this and does not finish it.
- Give each idea its own section, and keep the lesson, the check, the teach-back, and the record as separate steps the learner moves through at their own pace

Mayer 2023's pre-training principle backs naming terms first, and his segmenting principle backs learner-paced steps.

## Tie the concrete case to the rule

A concrete case alone teaches one instance, and a rule alone gives the learner nothing to hold it by.

- Show a concrete case beside the general rule it illustrates
- Say how each part of the case maps onto the rule, rather than leaving the mapping to the learner

The IES practice guide's Recommendation 4, rated moderate, backs connecting abstract and concrete representations of one concept. Whether the concrete case should come first and fade is a lead nobody opened, so this file sets no order.
